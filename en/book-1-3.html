Part Three: The Love of Helen

Act Three of the Play

The Story of Helen's Love

Densius is Pygmalion's friend. His wife's name is Ira, and they have a daughter named Angel. Ira has invited Pygmalion and his sister to a gathering to introduce them to a beautiful young woman named Helen, with the intention that Pygmalion might ask for her hand in marriage. This is part of the story. Ira describes Helen as follows:

She is a mythical being who has emerged from the depths of history; a legendary figure over whom blood has been shed, whose city has been conquered, and around whom cries of grief and shouts of triumph have risen into the air. Her name is Helen. Helen is a mythical and enchanting beauty, whose spell lies entirely in her beauty.

She is a charming, graceful, fair-skinned girl, delicate and slender, somewhat smaller, shorter, and more fragile than I am. Her skin is moon-white, like the whiteness of a pearl—one of Pygmalion's beloveds, Pearl. Her hair is golden, slightly curly, and long, and her eyes are green like emeralds. Her fair complexion and eyes resemble Pearl's, although Helen's face is somewhat fuller and broader, and her lips are more prominent and larger than Pearl's. Nevertheless, her complexion is as white as a pearl.

Her hands and figure are small and delicate. She is almost like a doll designed for dancing, charm, and enchantment. She loves dancing and gymnastics, and her dream is to shine on the stages of the Greek Olympic Games as a gymnast. She is quiet and always smiling; witty, humorous, cheerful, unpretentious, and free-spirited. She gives little thought to judging the goodness or wickedness of others and prefers to remain in motion, like restless waves along the shore that never seem to settle.

In a word, she is the daughter of the fairy king. Now, Pygmalion, sculptor, are you so eager to conquer this impregnable fortress, this lady of the legendary city of love? Or do you possess such artistry and skill that you can calm this restless heart in your embrace? Helen herself?!

Ira sighed and said softly, in a tender voice: "Ah! Whatever I say, I cannot do her justice! And, as the saying goes, hearing about something is never the same as seeing it."

Beryl began to clap gently, then more enthusiastically. Pygmalion joined in. Beryl said, "You have passed the theatrical rehearsal with flying colors! Well done! The way you have described her has made me eager to see her, let alone Pygmalion!"

By the time Pygmalion encounters Helen, he has reached a new level of intellectual and emotional maturity. He has changed from a silent man, unable to respond when faced with Artemisia's love, into an eloquent lover with remarkable verbal skill and an artist's power to create vivid mental images.

The entire phenomenon of Abulomovism—the helplessness, awkwardness, and speechlessness that once overcame him before the love of a respected young woman—has now disappeared. Through his passionate words of love, he transports Helen into an imaginary world. With his net and his trap, he draws her into a delicate, interwoven sphere, turns her gently in his hands, and uses his penetrating gaze to lead her into the land of love and dreams.

The psychological effect of Pygmalion now manifests itself in him. He does not treat Helen merely as a gymnast and acrobat, but as a queen seated beside Emperor Pygmalion in the great palaces, public squares, and amphitheatres of Rome and Greece. Together, they watch and judge the performances of young gymnasts and acrobats, each displaying their abilities in an effort to please Helen.

The legendary Helen, a magnificent queen, offers only a delicate smile. Beside Pygmalion, she feels successful, happy, and fulfilled. Soon their love becomes mutual and passionate: one soul in two forms, one spirit in two bodies.

This time, Pygmalion is no longer helpless or silent. He rides alongside Helen, urges their horses into a gallop, asks for her hand in marriage, and wins the full approval of her parents. He has become a capable man of action who takes his dream Helen to his palace and tells her stories that transport her to the city of Troy, where Helen is a princess connected with two lands.

Pygmalion recounts and reinterprets the legend of Helen for her. In his company, Helen's spirit rises, and her heart fills with passion and excitement. She experiences a genuine and unselfish love, the kind that can captivate and enchant any young woman.

But soon the boundaries between imagination and reality, sleep and dreams, and the life actually being lived begin to disappear. An invitation arrives from the Roman court for Helen and the other dancers, acrobats, and gymnasts of Paphos in Greece. They are invited to travel to Rome aboard the emperor's magnificent royal ship and perform before the imperial court.

They are promised the emperor's attention and favor, numerous golden gifts, the highest distinction in gymnastics and acrobatics, and other rewards that could transform their lives completely.

Helen discusses the invitation with Pygmalion, who responds with enthusiasm. He has always been caring and supportive of her. They consider travelling together, or allowing Helen to go alone and arranging a splendid wedding upon her return, one that would fill the entire city of Paphos with excitement and celebration.

However, after the ship has set sail, Helen is falsely told that Pygmalion cannot accompany her. When the vessel has travelled far from the shore, she discovers that she has been deceived. Had she and Pygmalion been formally married, he could have travelled with her.

Upon learning the truth, Helen realizes that it is already too late: the ship is far from the coast, and she cannot return. She faints and falls into a coma.

But what happens next? Does she forget Pygmalion? Does she never regain consciousness and eventually die? Does the legendary Helen return in triumph and marry Pygmalion, remaining with him to this day? Or do they grow weary of one another and part ways?

Who has Helen been in your own life? Do you have a mental image of your own Helen? After reading the complete story, what problems do you see in it, and how might it be improved? Has a Helen ever crossed your path? What was her story?
