Part Two: The Love Story of Artemisia

Act Two of the Novel

The Love Story of Artemisia

The entire sky of Pygmalion's imagination is filled with clouds of dreams and memories of Pearl. With love and passion, he creates dozens of graceful statues and beautiful portraits of her. Some he sells, while others he places in halls, above entrances, and along the passageways of buildings. One of the most beautiful and delicate statues stands beneath an arch among the seven arches of his palace. It is as though he gradually wishes to consign Pearl to the arch of oblivion in the hall of his mind. Little by little, her memories begin to fade. At the same time, clouds of lethargy, boredom, passivity, and inaction gather across his inner sky. Then the love of Artemisia arrives.

The love of Artemisia is a story of love at first sight. Their eyes meet for a moment, and Pygmalion is lost. He has fallen in love. For the first time, he raises his head and discovers that there is a being called a woman in this world! The difference between this love and his love for Pearl is that Pygmalion has now entered the realm of maturity. The childish, tender, and compassionate affection he felt for Pearl has given way to a different passion and intensity for Artemisia.

In Pygmalion's eyes, Artemisia is a sacred being, a goddess descended from the heavens who has set foot upon the earth with dignity and pride. She appears so magnificent, noble, and beautiful that his imagination gives her an ethereal, legendary form. This is precisely why, when he encounters Artemisia, he loses his composure. He becomes helpless, lifeless, speechless, and numb.

Pygmalion's love for Artemisia recalls a condition that might be called Oblomovism, after the novel Oblomov by Ivan Goncharov. Oblomov is a pampered young landowner who inherits wealth from a feudal and aristocratic family in Russia, at a time when the foundations of that society are beginning to weaken. Confronted with social contradictions, he remains motionless, incapable of taking action or summoning the determination to change his stagnant life. His passivity reflects, in part, the stagnation of the society in which he lives.

When Oblomov encounters Olga, an intelligent and practical young woman, he cannot overcome his own helplessness. He sinks into a swamp of indecision and inaction. Perhaps some form of Oblomovism exists in all our lives at certain moments: the forces within us and the forces acting upon us cancel one another out, leaving us motionless at a point of zero.

Oblomov is a romantic love story. Oblomov falls in love with a beautiful young woman named Olga. For days, months, and hours, he sits beneath a tree opposite her illuminated window, absorbed in dreams and fantasies about her. In heat, cold, and rain, he remains beneath that tree, gazing at the window on the upper floor. He lives in his own imagination and in the imagined world of Olga, even though she is only a few steps and a few stairs away and is far more accessible than he believes.

The story of Oblomov is a beautiful romance, but it is also a psychological portrait of helplessness, inadequacy, inertia, and the inability to act when confronted with love and life.

Unlike Oblomov, a pampered aristocratic landowner, Pygmalion has spent his entire life as a capable and practical man of action. He has helped himself and others escape illusions and dead ends. At times, he has taken groups of fifty young men and women on outings to gardens and historical sites around ancient Paphos in Greece, without showing romantic interest in any of the young women.

Yet now he has become lost in the city of Artemisia's eyes. Her love and her eyes seem to have enchanted him. He is distressed, helpless, tongue-tied, and unable to act. For hours, day and night, he thinks only of her. His sole consolation is the brief moments when Artemisia passes along the route from the city's art hall to her home. He waits for the chance to see her, even if only for a moment, without finding the courage to speak to her or make the slightest gesture.

Days and months pass. Pygmalion finds a new subject for his sculpture. He spends these days and months carving her graceful figure, beautiful face, and bright eyes. Where will this Oblomovism of Pygmalion lead him?

Where will my journey take me,

drunk with your unrestrained intoxication?

(Rumi)

Will he attain union with Artemisia? Are there obstacles that stand in the way? Can there be a love more innocent and pure than Pygmalion's love for Artemisia? Will they reach one another and sustain their love? Or will the storm of Pygmalion's passion crash upon the ground amid the mirages of disappointment, its heat and fervour evaporating into the sky?

Perhaps they will be united, only to discover after a time that their differences are incompatible and that these differences will wither the green garden of their life and carry it away. These are among the possibilities explored in the story of Artemisia and Pygmalion.

As you read this story, what criticisms might you offer? How could the story be improved? Has there ever been an Artemisia in your life before whom you became speechless, numb, and motionless, as though enchanted or possessed? What image of Artemisia lives in your imagination? What did her face look like, and what was the city of her eyes like?

Poems Pygmalion Wrote for His Artemisia

Here are some of the poems Pygmalion composed for his beloved Artemisia, with all the passion and emotion of his heart:

My Artemisia, you are the sovereign of my heart.

You are the utmost beauty in all the universe!


Who can comprehend the glory of such beauty

if not I?

Who can honour and protect such beauty

if not I?


What is the price of your beauty

and the tenderness of your heart?

Nothing but my humble life!


What can repay the brilliance of your beautiful eyes

but a love burning with passion?

Who can defend your precious being

to the very end, if not I?


I shall protect you in the hidden layers of my heart.

For you, Pearl concealed within my heart,

I shall become a shell,

tossed by every wave,

so that I may bring you peace.

May I perish if I ever seek anything

other than your happiness!

O Artemisia of my stories,

recognize me and let me enter your unknown island,

where each day I may discover a new jewel

across your vast and wondrous land.


Call me into the rainbow of your eyes,

so that, like a prism,

I may find a new colour there each day.


Draw my eyes toward your magnificent sky,

so that every night I may discover a new star.


Invite me into the city of your eyes,

where the light in your eyes becomes my joy,

and the sorrow in your heart becomes mine.


Draw me into the vastness of your beautiful world,

so that each day I may offer you

a new interpretation of its wonders.


Ask me to remain beside you,

so that each night I may fall asleep

counting the rhythm of your breaths.

O my love without end,

you are the light of my morning!

You are the companion of my dark nights!


You are the shining candle,

and I circle the light

you kindle within my heart.


I shall become a butterfly,

emerging from my cocoon

and spreading my wings toward you.


The opening of my wings for flight and ascent

comes from the fragrance of your affection

that spreads upon the wind.


I shall entrust the passion of my love

and the longing to see you to the clouds,

so that, with a gentle and loving rain of mist,

they may reveal the secret of my heart to you.


I shall tell my secret to the wind,

so that the morning breeze may carry it to you.


“O wind, tell my tale in secret;

tell the secret of my heart in a hundred tongues.

Speak, but not in a way that wearies the beloved;

say a word, and weave it into your tale.”

(Hafez)
