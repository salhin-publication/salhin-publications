Part One: The Love of Morvarid

The First Act: The Story of Morvarid's Love

This part follows the beginning of Pygmalion's story, exploring love and the development of his character. It introduces readers to his childhood, his family, the stories that shape his imagination, and the characters' perspectives on love and life.

The story of Morvarid's love represents the first step in Pygmalion's gradual development in understanding and experiencing love. Morvarid is Pygmalion's cousin and, together with his younger sister, one of his childhood companions. The three grow up together and eventually reach adolescence and adulthood. During childhood, they spend much of their time together. They sleep beside Morvarid's doll, and each of them tells little stories to help the doll fall asleep. Their days are filled with happiness and play.

Then adolescence arrives, and the freedom of their companionship becomes more restricted. Eventually, Morvarid's family moves to another city, and separation comes. In understanding the complicated yet sometimes simple relationships of life, Morvarid has advanced beyond Pygmalion because she is a girl and has begun to perceive the emotional signals between them. How will she use her behaviour and glances to attract Pygmalion's love?

Pygmalion, however, is still living in the world of childhood. He does not yet understand or feel the powerful, passionate love that is beginning to emerge around him. These experiences form part of a series of meaningful and delightful events whose details can be explored in the full novel.

The passage out of childhood gains depth and meaning through overcoming imagined fears and developing the power of childhood imagination. Stories of spirits, fairies, ghosts, love, courtship, and the captivating beauty of the heroines in a grandmother's tales all contribute to this process. How does the hero of a grandmother's story reach his beautiful and elusive beloved? What obstacles and dangers must he overcome? What actions must he avoid? How should he behave when he meets the beautiful woman he loves?

These stories also ask what gives life its meaning, and how love, devotion, and the journey toward a beloved give a hero's life purpose. What becomes of such heroic efforts in the end? These are some of the questions that emerge in the first part of the novel.

The old woman said: "There are rules to travelling the path of love. First, you must learn them; second, you must observe them. And third, when you reach your beloved, there will be other rules that you will learn at that time. Then another teacher will guide you—not only through the sweetness of her words, but also through the arch of her eyebrows, the tresses of her hair, and her grace, charm, and coquettish gestures..."

Every movement of the beautiful woman now revealed another aspect of beauty. Her laughter brought a new and unparalleled play of lines to her joyful face. Every gesture was a delight to behold. She was not merely a motionless statue; she was dynamic, like the waves of the sea. She embodied a world of beauty and a wave of delicacy and tenderness, accompanied by compassion and kindness.

...This was part of the grandmother's tale. That was how the grandmother was: this was only one of her hundreds of stories. She carried the children into the world of tales and legends, an ethereal and imaginary world full of hope, dreams, beauty, and devotion. In that world, the dead came back to life and demons and monsters were bound. They floated in an ocean of imagination, in another realm known as the unseen world, where anything was possible and whatever could not happen in the visible world could come true.

It was a land of multi-headed serpents and dragons breathing fire, of the Simurgh, flying carpets, love, hope, and beautiful women. Then the grandmother brought them back to reality. Yet they returned having reached the fulfilment of their imagination, and once again had to live on this earthly ground. What a world childhood is! Such was the grandmother.

...And the mother said: "Art is what a woman needs. The art of loving and being loved, the art of being praised and adored—as though a heavenly goddess had placed a part of herself within the being called woman. Satisfaction in praise and adoration: and a part within man—the art of admiring and praising a woman, and captivating her through art and ability."

...Art is a land of spiritual elevation, with springs that quench the human thirst for understanding and experiencing beauty and wonder.

This was enough to bring order to Pygmalion's being. His thoughts became steadier, and his will became a powerful driving force.

...A woman's love is like a climbing vine. Women are often more realistic than people imagine, contrary to the common belief that they are dreamers and fantasists. The vine's growth is limited: it seeks a tall tree around which to climb toward the sky. For women, what can be reached is real, rather than what exists only in fantasy and dreams and cannot be attained. Like a vine creeping along the ground and wrapping itself around the nearest tree, it adapts to what is within reach. Nature has made women accommodating and conciliatory; they are symbols of union and fertility. Men and women differ. If women were exactly like men, union would be more difficult.

What wise lessons are hidden in these stories? How must Pygmalion and his sister, Berlian, develop their abilities in order to enjoy a life filled with love and happiness? What do these lessons have to do with finding Pygmalion's beautiful beloved and learning how to behave and live with her? These delightful stories carry Pygmalion into a world of dreams and sweet imaginings. But what happens in real life?

Morvarid is close at hand, living in the family home. In Pygmalion's imagination and vision of the future, there is no beloved more beautiful, noble, or lovable than she. But can he bring this companion, already so close to him, into the home and magnificent palace he has prepared for her in his mind? Or will he become an example of the person whose beloved is at home while he wanders the world, like someone who has water in a pitcher but remains thirsty?

What obstacles can arise between a person and a beloved who seems so close and attainable? Can Pygmalion reach Morvarid, with whom he has spent more than twelve years of his life? Will they finally be united, only to discover that the lasting love they seek was the love of childhood itself? These are some of the questions concealed within the first act and the first part, the story of Morvarid's love.

The love of Morvarid and Pygmalion is one of those loves that may remain beside us for years, accompanying us through life, while we fail to recognise, understand, or properly respond to it. Perhaps one reason is that we have not yet reached physical or emotional maturity; perhaps there are dozens of other reasons why we overlook what is near and available to us. When do we finally recognise it? Only after it has gone far away from us?

If we attain the love we seek, will it endure, or will it falter halfway and return us to the same immaturity and childhood from which we hoped to escape? Who was your cousin or childhood companion? Have you had a Morvarid in your life? What did she look like? What is your image of Morvarid's beauty and stature? What memories do you share with her? You may write to me about this, or send me your own imagined picture of your Morvarid.

This part continues Pygmalion's journey and experiences through stories and reflections on Morvarid, family, emotional relationships, the power of storytelling, and the role of love in shaping thought and feeling.
