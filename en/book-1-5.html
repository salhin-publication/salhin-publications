Part Five

Act Five of the Play

The Love Story of Cratia

From And This Is the Book of Love: Galatea and Pygmalion

The Attraction of Love

Fate had written another episode into Pygma's life: an experience of paralysing attraction radiating from a being named Cratia.

A few kilometres from the city of Paphos, on the slope of a mountain and atop a green hill, stood a separate urban district. Its inhabitants followed customs resembling those of the peoples of Central Asia, which at that time belonged to the realm of Persia, and in some respects they also resembled the people of China. The residents of this district were called the Xenos, or the Xenos people—a name associated with strangers, guests, travellers, and visitors. They came from a distant land and followed their own particular customs, although no one knew exactly what those customs were. In Paphos at that time, everyone could be considered a stranger, but these people were strangers who had deliberately separated themselves and their community from the city's other peoples.

And now, some passages from the love story of Cratia:

The middle-aged man, with a long black beard and a short moustache, met Pygmalion's eyes, greeted him, and stepped forward.

"Greetings. My name is Ghoras, and I am one of the people of Xenos. I have heard a great deal about your abilities. I have a request: please honour us by carving a statue for the entrance to our city. If you permit me, I would prefer that you come alone, Your Excellency, and carry out the work yourself. There is no need for an assistant. My family will remain by your side. Would that be possible?"

Pygma fell silent for a moment, considering the request. Then he replied, "It is possible, but the carving will take a long time under those circumstances."

"You will certainly come," Ghoras replied. "You are an unrivalled master."

He then took out a piece of painted cloth, unrolled it like a scroll, and held it before Pygmalion. "This is the figure of the man who is our spiritual and historical leader."

Pygmalion took the cloth and walked towards the tall windows to examine the image carefully. The man's facial features resembled Ghoras's own: a long, pointed beard and a short moustache that seemed to have been shaved, leaving only a narrow line of hair. He had sharp cheekbones, a triangular face, penetrating eyes, and a rounded hat with a flat centre and an elevated crown.

On Monday, Pygmalion arrived at the arched entrance to that district of the city, the place its inhabitants called the City of Xenos.

Ghoras and his wife were farmers who worked in the fields from morning until night. They had prepared a sleeping room for Pygma so that, throughout the long period during which he would carve their spiritual leader's statue, he could stay in their house and workshop. Since he often worked late into the night, his sleeping quarters were beside the workshop.

They had instructed their elder daughter, Cratia, and their younger daughter, Nikokatia, who was only five years old, to provide Pygma with the water, food, and drinks he needed. They were to serve as his assistants, much as his own sisters might have done. The girls' brothers were all married and lived away from the family. They worked with their father in the nearby fields.

Pygmalion had a reserved, almost stony manner when it came to the looks and attention of women and girls. He would glance at someone only once, and his head would usually remain bowed. Hundreds of women may have crossed his path, yet none had managed to capture his attention. He imagined that only a few exceptional women belonged to the galaxy in which he lived, while all the others belonged to a different galaxy.

Cratia arrived. The young woman placed a tray beside the worktable where the tools lay. As she did so, she looked Pygma up and down, taking in his tall stature, his soft and delicate appearance, and his small, gentle hands and features.

"Hello, Pygma!" she said. Turning to her father, she added, "Father, what a jeweller you have found!" She smiled and laughed, then faced Pygmalion. "You are even better than all the praise I have heard about you. Tell me, are you a stonemason or a jeweller?"

Pygma had been working and paying little attention to his visitors. Her words surprised him, and he began to examine the young woman with a brief, sidelong glance, from her feet to her face and the headscarf she wore.

He had never before received such an informal, warm, and heartfelt greeting from either a man or a woman. How extraordinary! Women and girls who had little contact with society usually greeted people with modesty and carefully measured words, especially when addressing a stranger they had never met—and even more so when their father was present. Yet here was Cratia, speaking and laughing with such ease.

Her father, laughing warmly, said, "Pygma, these are my daughters. They will assist you while you work on the statue. My elder daughter is named Nikokratia, and my younger daughter is Nikokatia. My wife, my sons, and I will be away during the day because we work in the fields. These girls will assist you and act as your sisters during your stay. Do not stand on ceremony with them. Make yourself comfortable. Whatever you need—towels, cloth, or any other necessities—ask them."

Beauty is a light radiating from the sun of the universe.

Cratia's Appearance

For a few moments, Pygma gazed at her face and head. Her hair was braided on both sides, with one strand rising above the collar. Her ears were very small and set on either side of her head, partly covered by her braids. Her face was small and round, slightly tilted and oval, ending in a pointed chin. Her cheeks were full yet elongated. Her lips, naturally red and full of colour, resembled a tiny rosebud.

The lower line of her lips curved towards the hollow beneath her small, pointed, slightly protruding chin, where a dimple was visible in the centre. Her eyebrows were extraordinarily arched and slender. Her eyes were black as night, elongated and large—larger than usual. A dark line of kohl extended along her eyelids and lashes, making them appear even longer. Perhaps her eyes resembled those of the women of the Pharaohs, whose kohl extended along both the upper and lower eyelids.

Her nose was small. Her forehead was high, prominent, slightly raised, and shining. Her teeth were tiny and whiter than pearls. Her lips were small and rosebud-like, neither too large nor too thin, yet distinctly full. Their expression was innocent and kind.

Her face had a childlike quality. Whenever she laughed, her shell-white teeth shone and her eyes sparkled. Her long nose, elongated chin, prominent cheeks, and slender, long neck gave her face a special dignity and beauty. It was a wild, untamed beauty, accompanied by pride and firmness—a kind of absolute certainty and stubbornness. Her pride resembled that of a three- or four-year-old girl who, when angered, turns her face away and withdraws in proud silence.

Her face was slightly triangular, with a small chin and full lips. Her almond-shaped eyes were penetrating, and her eyebrows formed graceful arches visible from the angle of her face. The line of her eyes was clearly defined, with a noticeable prominence above them and a broad space beneath the eyebrows. Her forehead was high and prominent, curving towards her hairline. The area between her eyebrows was flat, and her ears were visible. Long hair framed her head, with a few strands falling across her high forehead.

Beauty and attraction were mingled in her, but attraction was the stronger of the two.

Cratia possessed a striking and distinctive appearance: a different kind of dazzling beauty belonging to a young woman descended from the ancient tribes of the Far East and Central Asia. It was a wild, untouched beauty.

Pygma had never encountered such attraction before—an attraction that seemed to pierce straight through him. It was another rare manifestation of the goddess of the universe appearing on Earth. No writer could adequately describe her, and no painter or sculptor could bring together so much delicacy, beauty, and pride in a single face.

Many young men among the Xenos admired her, yet her imposing presence and pride left them scarcely able to express their feelings. She seemed like another manifestation of a celestial goddess who had descended to Earth and now bestowed her affection upon Pygma, leaving him lost in contemplation of her.

She was like a captivating infant with an angelic face, the kind of beautiful baby one longs to hold close against one's chest. Pygma had seen how passionately his sisters embraced their babies, pressing them beneath their necks and against their breasts with tenderness and warmth. Seeing Cratia stirred a similar feeling in him, yet her dignity and imposing presence would not even allow him to look directly at her face.

Whenever he saw her, he lowered his head or looked obliquely at a spot on the ground. Cratia interpreted this manner of looking as a sign of Pygmalion's piety and would murmur to herself, "What a pure young man!"

Pygma wondered what kind of magic or enchantment could exist in the world. Surely, he thought, such things were all fantasies and illusions. Yet the real enchantment stood before him. Beauty itself was the magic that poured the elixir of love into the heart, while its heat appeared in a face reddened with embarrassment.

What a delicate and gentle being she was! What extraordinary eyes had come into existence! She was a miracle. Everyone searches for miracles, yet the very eye through which we look upon existence is itself a miracle. Pygma was even more astonished by the artistry of creation than by her appearance.

Pygma had not intended to open a window in his heart to romantic love for Cratia. Yet her respectful behaviour, courtesy, depth of meaning, vitality, and indescribable attraction began to melt the frozen ocean within him. Her influence was almost aggressive: it broke through the vast expanse of ice and advanced relentlessly.

Little by little, his stolen, hurried, and reverent glances turned into flames of light and warmth that shone upon Cratia's beautiful face, illuminating her like the moon on dark nights. He was a sun radiating the heat of love, while the moon's attraction had captured him. That attraction made him illuminate Cratia's beautiful and captivating face as though she were a shining moon.

Gradually, his ice melted. He began to wonder: why not? Why should there not be a bond between him and Cratia? Who could prevent the light of love's sun from shining when the existence of the entire universe depends upon that light?

But how could Cratia, a daughter of a distant people with roots in forgotten history and ancient customs, ever marry a city-dwelling sculptor? At first, she had no doubt about the matter. Her heart was not a concrete wall, but iron—and yet she had developed a spiritual, emotional, and inward love for Pygma.

Time and again, in her passionate speeches, she would say, "Poor Pygmalion! For you, I am an impregnable fortress and a forbidden fruit. Remain content with this pleasant fantasy. Do not go beyond it."

Pygma did not understand the full meaning of her words. He replied, "Even your imagination and your teasing are virtues, my lady. The thought of conquering that fortress and smelling that forbidden fruit is delightful, too. Let me remain in this dream, then, and die within it."

Cratia, now captivated by the words of this newly arrived challenger, found that the joy of their conversation gave her confidence and reassurance. She said, "It is clear that the magic of your words is real and comes from a passionate heart."

At this stage, both Pygma and Cratia were doing the same thing: each was trying to discover which of them could ensnare the other's heart. Pygma was uncertain. His intentions were pure, and he hoped that he might enter the heart of this beautiful gazelle and perhaps find that fortune would allow them to form an eternal bond. But no one knew Cratia's true intentions.

Even when girls have no intention of entering into a relationship, they may enjoy being admired. Perhaps it increases their pride and confidence: "Look at what a beautiful being I am, with so many admirers!" If this illusory pride is not restrained, it can lead to ruin. They are not necessarily to blame; it is in their nature to appreciate being praised.

Women are shining moons: they spread beauty and light through the darkness. The sun seeks a moon upon which to cast its light—a moon that reflects that light and beauty. If there were no reflection from the moon, what need would there be for the sun's light?

Beauty is a light radiating from the sun of the universe.

Pygma remained uncertain, but Cratia had no doubt that no marriage would take place. Whatever existed between them, it was not lust. It was love—love itself.

For Pygma, this was not the kind of love in which one suddenly sees the face of a beloved and falls in love with her a hundredfold in a single instant, as in the fairy tales told by grandmothers.

An Image of Cratia

What, then, were Cratia's eyes really like? They resembled those of a beautiful gazelle: large and amber-coloured, a light brown containing tiny black specks, surrounded by a white sclera that became more visible whenever she turned her eyes. Their amber colour possessed the captivating quality of amber itself.

Between Pearl's green eyes and Roya's black eyes, this was an entirely different colour, one Pygma had never seen before—or perhaps had never noticed closely. Perhaps it was the most captivating and kindest eye colour of all!

But to whom did those eyes, that figure, and that tall, cypress-like stature belong? Who was Cratia?

Cratia was an unrivalled and fearless horsewoman. She was an intelligent and capable hunter, a skilled archer, and an expert with the lasso.

Among these farming and hunting people, the young men held competitions on days when there was no rain. These contests took place once a month, mostly during the summer, from late spring into early winter. They raced horses, threw lassos, and practised precision shooting at targets attached to trees or to raised objects a few metres above the ground.

The competitions were loud and tumultuous. The horses' hooves raised clouds of dust as the riders galloped about, challenging one another and practising the skills of warfare and hunting. Girls and boys alike took part. Remarkably, no distinction was made between the sexes in these activities.

One of their pursuits involved catching and riding falcons and training people in how to hunt with, or care for, falcons, eagles, gyrfalcons, and saker falcons.

The only person who disliked this activity was Cratia.

Her temperament was gentle, peaceful, and mild, like a cloud or mist slowly descending from a mountain and embracing everything and everyone it could reach with kindness, gentleness, and compassion. This was the very quality that had overwhelmed Pygma. It was one side of her personality—and the dominant one.

Yet she was also a sharp-clawed eagle, a fearsome cobra capable of bringing even a large animal to its knees with her venom. She was an archer whose accuracy surpassed that of every man and boy in her tribe. She made her own small, delicate arrows.

One day, while hunting on horseback, she had her bow drawn and aimed at a rabbit that an eagle was pursuing through the sky. Suddenly, she noticed a formidable eagle attacking her. With its beak and head forming a sharp, arrow-like point, it swooped towards her, apparently intending to blind her.

In that instant, while her bow was already drawn towards the rabbit, she redirected her aim at the eagle and released the arrow. The arrow passed through the eagle's eye and pinned its head to its body. The bird fell to the ground, astonishing and impressing the people of Xenos.

From that day onwards, they called Cratia "Eagle Eye." Warriors of the tribe, in addition to their given names, often carried another name or nickname.

But Cratia did not like this nickname at all. She regretted having caused the eagle's death. Everything had happened in a single moment. She often thought: if only she had ducked at that moment of excitement, or dropped her bow and covered her bent head with both forearms, the poor eagle might have pecked at her and flown away, still alive.

She felt that she was the one at fault. She had wanted to take the eagle's food. What was one rabbit worth when rabbits were so plentiful? A hundred rabbits would be a small price to pay for the life of a single eagle.

Instead of considering herself a hero, Cratia blamed herself for the eagle's death. Nevertheless, the incident established her reputation as a professional marksman and caused the young men of Xenos to admire her and fear her. They would never dare to mistreat her.

She rode alongside them at full gallop. Although her body was slender and she did not wrestle with them, she could match them in riding and hunting.

At night and at dusk, she would ride alone from her house to the farm, crossing the hillocks, ridges, narrow paths, and thickets between the fields. Beneath the moonlight, she carried food and other necessities to her family, who sometimes had to remain in the fields during summer nights to prevent wild boars from damaging the crops.

Although she hunted wild boar and rabbit without hesitation, she never took part in hunting deer, roe deer, red deer, or gazelles, nor did she have any interest in doing so.

At night, she galloped through the darkness like a shadow, a spirit, or a monster. No one dared to approach or harass her, and she feared no one.

Her constant companion was a black horse whose body seemed made of darkness. Its glossy black mane shone faintly in the night. The horse allowed no one else to ride it; only Cratia could mount it, and in her presence it was docile. It had won many horse races for her.

Such was Cratia: a wild horse, untamed and impossible to subdue.

Consequently, none of the young men in the city dared to approach her without her consent, much less establish a relationship with her, unless they could find a way to attract her attention. She did not consider any of them worthy of being her equal.

She had become a young woman of many dimensions—part city-dweller and part countrywoman—with ideas shaped in part by her ever-travelling brother.

Again and again, Cratia warned Pygma not to become attached to her. But how can anyone resist the attraction and gravitational pull of the moon? Even the vast oceans bow before the moon's gravity. Their waters respond to its pull, and the tides rise and fall.

How could anyone remain indifferent to such wild and indescribable beauty? And why, indeed, should one remain indifferent?

Yet Cratia, whose heart had at first been as cold and impenetrable as iron, gradually began to grow warm in response to Pygma's courtesy, reverence, knowledge, perfection, wisdom, and respectfulness in both word and deed.

The warmth began to spread throughout her being. She became lost in a vague astonishment and a painful uncertainty.

What events would this spiritual, inward, and meaningful love between them bring about?

The continuation of the story contains the tales that Cratia—the Scheherazade of Pygma's stories—tells to elevate Pygmalion's spirit and soul. Through this burning love, she teaches him social and cultural lessons, lessons about love and emotion, and a thousand subtleties finer than a strand of hair.

Cratia is a beautiful and captivating teacher. She could easily make Pygmalion swallow even the deadliest of poisons without his noticing—but why would she do such a thing? Instead, she pours the sweetest of honeys into his mouth, seeking to make her presence eternal in his spirit and soul.

She leaves him bewildered, mad, enchanted, delighted, and captive to her. What will happen? What will become of them?

The love story of Cratia and Pygmalion portrays the fullest maturity of two intelligent and wise individuals—intellectually, socially, physically, and psychologically. Between them, no veil should be necessary. But where will this beautiful and spiritual love finally lead?

If love has made their beings so transparent that no dark spot remains within them, where are the obstacles to their union? Is there any obstacle at all?

It is better to set speculation and conjecture aside and read the entire story and all the tales of Cratia.

Has there ever been a Cratia in your life? What image of Cratia—imagined or real—do you carry in your mind, or have you encountered in the world?

What do you think of this story? Could such a story actually happen, or is it merely a product of fantasy, unhealthy dream-making, and poetic imagination?
