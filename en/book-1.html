About the Book

About this book

And This Is the Book of Love approaches love from an international and human perspective. What is love? What views and definitions of love have human beings developed? What is the relationship between love and marriage? Is love merely sexual pleasure? What obstacles stand in the way of love between two people, love for humanity, and the moral and social advancement of human communities? Does love become more genuine and profound as a person's intellectual and emotional maturity develops? What is the relationship between personal growth, the advancement of human societies and civilization, and love?

What is idealized or imaginary love? Can it contribute to intellectual growth, deeper feelings, and emotional development? Just as physical growth occurs through different stages, are there also stages of intellectual and spiritual development in our understanding and experience of love? What psychological role can love play in bringing peace to the human mind and spirit? Can love become a driving force that awakens hidden human capacities and helps people build their lives? Does love give strength and create life? Just as love can help build the foundations of a couple's life, can it also contribute to the foundations of an ideal society and the higher aspirations of humanity? How can love become universal?

What is beauty, and why does everyone have a different understanding of it? Is beauty itself a virtue? What forms of love are illusory or unhealthy? Can love sometimes become a psychological illness? What is the relationship between purity, virtue, self-restraint, and genuine love? Is love present in every particle of existence? What role do stories, fiction, and novels play in shaping human imagination and thought?

Did human development take a new direction when people began recounting their experiences, telling stories, and passing their memories from one generation to the next? Is there a meaningful difference between our minds and those of Plato, or between our feelings and the feelings of Plato and Aristotle? Which aspects of human knowledge are expressed through stories? What is the relationship between art and love, and between reason and love? Where does the highest spiritual enjoyment of love lead?

Can love heal many personal and social troubles, or is this merely poetic exaggeration? How does exaggeration influence people and motivate them to act? What is destiny, and which factors shape it? What roles do freedom and choice play in human development? What is the relationship between independence of spirit and the development of love? These are among the many questions explored in this book.

The novel portrays the development of a young sculptor named Pygmalion. Across seven stages of love, it moves from childhood tales and surreal imagination to social events and the obstacles that hinder social progress. It is filled with stories of love and fidelity, advice and reflection, and attempts to find ways out of intellectual, psychological, and social dead ends.

The Story of Love

There is only one story of love, and yet how strange: from everyone I hear it, it is always new. —Hafez

This is the book of love. It is a story of love and devotion, exhilaration and separation, longing and reunion, peace and restlessness. It is about the continuity of life and existence, the many manifestations of the beloved, the beauty of form and expression, and the heart that remains eager, trembling, and restless for the beloved. It is a remembrance of the voice of love.

I have found nothing sweeter than the sound of love, a remembrance that may endure beneath this revolving dome.

The myth of Pygmalion is one of the most compelling stories of ancient Greece. It has inspired artists, writers, and thinkers throughout history. The story concerns Pygmalion, a skilled sculptor from Cyprus, whose perfectionism and devotion to art lead him to fall in love not with an ordinary human being, but with a work of art he has created himself.

The story of Galatea and Pygmalion is presented here as a story of love that belongs to all humanity. Each of us carries within ourselves an image of a unique and beautiful being, an image that seems to have been placed in our existence before we consciously begin searching for it. Our restlessness may arise from separation from that imagined ideal and the desire to find it. For each person, Galatea and Pygmalion may represent a distinctive figure suited to that person's inner world. We spend much of our lives searching for this image. Even when we find a beloved and live happily together, the heart may still feel that life has passed in the blink of an eye. Why does the heart remain restless? Perhaps because we continue to carry an ideal image within our spirit, and our journey toward love continues.

The story of Pygmalion and Galatea transcends time and place. Although it draws on an ancient Greek myth, it is also a universal story that can speak to peoples and cultures across borders. This version adopts a cosmopolitan perspective in the hope of reaching readers everywhere. It is a human story: a story of love and devotion, a story that belongs to every age and every society.

The story of love is the story of all of us. As you read this book, you may remember earlier days, the love or loves you have experienced, the excitement of youth, the feeling of freedom, and the hope that love might last forever.

Younger readers may instead wonder how they should respond to love, now or in the future, so that their experience becomes a meaningful memory for the rest of their lives.

Love is an essential part of human existence. This is the story of Pygmalion's love for Galatea. What is your own story of love?

Earlier writers have offered their own interpretations of this myth. Each storyteller has approached it from a particular view of the world. Future generations may tell it differently in other places and times. This, however, is the author's own interpretation.

In the earliest well-known literary version, written by the Roman poet Ovid in his work Metamorphoses, Pygmalion fails to find a woman who meets his expectations. Disappointed, he creates a statue of a woman according to his own ideal. He falls in love with the figure that emerges from his imagination, which he has carved, refined, and adorned with his own hands.

In the nineteenth century, the English writer Bernard Shaw offered another interpretation in the context of the class structure of his time. His work explored social status and the conventions of the British upper classes. In Iran, Sadegh Hedayat wrote a darker interpretation in his story The Doll Behind the Curtain, exploring isolation, illusion, and the emptiness that may result from extreme withdrawal from society. The point is not simply to label Hedayat's view as nihilistic; rather, the story shows how isolation and excessive individualism can lead to emptiness.

The French painter and sculptor Jean-Léon Gérôme also drew inspiration from the myth. His work Pygmalion and Galatea, created in 1890, is held by the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York. The artwork depicts the sculptor's intense love for the figure he has created.

These earlier interpretations form part of humanity's shared cultural heritage. This novel offers a realistic yet romantic approach to the myth and introduces philosophical and mystical reflections on love. It suggests that each of us carries an ideal image within, and that we may spend our lives searching for a person who resembles that image. When reality does not match the ideal, we may try to change ourselves, our relationship, or our understanding of the beloved. People can influence one another profoundly, helping one another grow toward greater maturity and understanding.

From this perspective, marriage can be understood as a workshop of sculpture: a place where attachment to outward beauty may gradually develop into appreciation for inner beauty, emotional depth, character, and moral qualities.

In human life, imagination and reality meet. An ideal can become part of a real relationship through effort, experience, and mutual growth. A perfect relationship does not simply exist ready-made; it requires sustained work to connect one's ideals with the realities of life.

Life is a journey of discovery. We first imagine a beautiful and unique figure in the workshop of our own hearts, then hope to meet that person and find happiness together. Our journey through life may also become a journey toward a wider understanding of love.

Pygmalion, disappointed in his search for a beloved, continues to develop through each encounter with love. He hopes to form a clearer image of the person he seeks, to carve that image from marble, and to bring it to life through the power of art and imagination. He dreams that this unique figure may free him from the coldness and hardships of the world and lead him toward a happier future.

This collection includes stories and episodes for adults, young people, and adolescents. It may inspire younger readers in their understanding of love and life; one day, they may also read its stories to their own children, helping them fall asleep and enriching their imaginations.

Alongside poetry, adventure, and philosophical and mystical reflections, the book includes tales of spirits, fairies, ghosts, and the story-world of a grandmother. It challenges ignorance and condemns cruelty, violence, betrayal, hatred, greed, and envy. It is also an effort to understand love, mutual understanding, and tolerance as paths toward peace and lasting affection.

As in Ovid's myth, this story takes place in an imagined era before formal religions had become established in Greece and Rome. The goddesses Venus, Aphrodite, Hera, and Athena belong to the mythology and cultural imagination of that world, while different peoples maintained their own traditions and beliefs.

What I say is measured by your understanding; I die longing for true understanding. —Rumi
